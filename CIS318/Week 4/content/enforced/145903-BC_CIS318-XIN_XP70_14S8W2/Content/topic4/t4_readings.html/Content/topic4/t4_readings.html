Freedom of Expression

Freedom of expression is based on the First Amendment that protects the right to freedom of religion and freedom of expression. Court decisions have broadened the definition of speech, under expression, to include nonverbal, visual, and symbolic forms of expression.

Think About This

The onset and pervasive use of the Internet has definitely had a lasting impact on the First Amendment. The Internet and computer technology in general have pushed the limits of the First Amendment in many areas.

Take a moment and think of at least one area in which the Internet has raised ethical issues around the First Amendment in defining certain areas of free speech or free expression. Consider the following areas:

Anonymity – How and when is anonymity protected?

Pornography – Who controls who sees it, and what defines something as pornography?

Defamation – Who or what defines the interpretation of words and phrases?

Intellectual Property

What is it that earns Microsoft a profit? The obvious answer would be the software and the underlying source code for such products as MS Office. Add to this Microsoft’s popular hardware products such as Xbox, computer peripherals, and the like. Who owns that source code? Why does the team of programmers that developed the source code not own the code? Is source code similar to the secret Coca-Cola formula? All of these questions lead us into the area of intellectual property. According to Reynolds (2010),

Intellectual property is a term used to describe works of the mind – such as art, books, films, formulas, inventions, music, and processes – that are distinct, and owned or created by a single person or group. Intellectual property is protected through copyright, patent, and trade secret laws. (p. 197)

All of us have intellectual property that belongs to us. Think about and list the papers, articles, products, etc. you have created in your lifetime that only you own. The list is probably quite lengthy. You have already created intellectual property in the form of the three papers you have written in this course.

In business, intellectual property is also known as a trade secret. The secret Coca-Cola formula, the Colonel’s secret blend of herbs and spices (Kentucky Fried Chicken), and Microsoft’s Windows source code are all trade secrets. What constitutes a trade secret? Although a difficult term to define, trade secrets are generally understood to be information used in the conduct of a business and not commonly known by others. Following are six factors used to determine if information is protected as a trade secret:

  1. The extent to which the information is known outside the business.
  2. The degree to which employees and others involved in the business know it.
  3. The measures required to guard the secrecy of the information.
  4. The value of the information to the business and its competitors.
  5. The amount of effort required to develop the information.
  6. The ease with which the information can be acquired or duplicated by others.
