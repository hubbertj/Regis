Computer and Internet Crime

The increasing complexity of the computing environment, higher user expectations, expanding systems, and increased reliance on software with known vulnerabilities have lead to a 25-fold increase in the number of reported IT security incidents over the past five years.

IT professionals and the businesses for which they work must take action in response to security incidents such as those caused by viruses, worms, Trojan Horses, or Denial-of-Service attacks. Examples of such actions are notification, protection of evidence and security logs, containment, eradication, and follow-up. Businesses must ensure they learn from incidents in an effort to avoid future incidents or at least to minimize and contain these incidents.

Privacy

Like many other concepts introduced during this course, the concept of privacy is also difficult to define. Privacy is considered a human right and something to be valued. In the workplace, privacy concerns are paramount with emerging technologies that can literally spy on employees.

Three definitions of privacy have emerged:

  1. The right to be left alone.
  2. The right to have control over personal information.
  3. The right not to have undocumented personal information known by others.

Yet, just because privacy is a need, this does not make it justifiable. Two examples of arguments regarding the value of privacy come from our study of utilitarianism and Kantian methods. The utilitarian argument would take a consequences approach. The Kantian argument would deal with respect for persons.

Balancing employee rights to privacy and employer concerns about productivity is not easy. Four factors for consideration are:

  1. What kind of information is the employer gathering?
  2. What is the employer doing with this information?
  3. Who inside the company has access to this information?
  4. Who outside of the company has access to this information?
