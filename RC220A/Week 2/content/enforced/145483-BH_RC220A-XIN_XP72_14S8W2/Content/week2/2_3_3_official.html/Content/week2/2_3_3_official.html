2.3.3 An Official Definition of Hinduism

Due to pressures, both internal and external, this loosely connected web of beliefs became a problem. Internally, in the twentieth century, there was a great demand for social change. The caste system came under great scrutiny. And as the world became a smaller place due to the escalating ease of travel, and the electronic rapid exchange of information, the diversity that is Hinduism became a problem for outsiders.

In order to clarify and define Hinduism, the Indian Supreme Court formally defined Hindu beliefs in a way that affirms universality rather than exclusiveness According to the ruling, to be Hindu means:

  1. Acceptance and reverence of Vedas as the foundation of Hindu philosophy;
  2. A spirit of tolerance, and willingness to understand and appreciate others point of view, recognizing that truth has many sides;
  3. Acceptance of belief that vast cosmic periods of creation, maintenance and dissection continuously occur;
  4. Acceptance of belief in reincarnation;
  5. Recognition that paths to truth and salvation are many;
  6. Recognition that there may be numerous god and goddesses to worship, without necessarily believing in worship through idols;
  7. Unlike other religions, absence of belief in a specific set of philosophical concepts. (Fisher, 2002, p. 125, 127)
This summary of beliefs is the basis of Hinduism and is clearly the foundational understanding upon which their worldview is based.
