2.5 Spiritual Principles in Hindu Daily Life

It is certainly true that Hinduism has provided the broad cultural and religious framework that has held India together despite its astonishing linguistic, ethnic and political diversity and divisions. Hinduism is an essential for understanding Indian culture and civilization.

Keep the light shining - By Karan Singh

Indian life is highly structured. With the adoption of the Laws of Manu c 200 C.E. religion and society were inextricably entwined. This text set out the procedures for men progressing through life in terms of the individual's personal focus, responsibilities toward family and obligations to society. The role of women was codified as subservient to men and assigned to exclusive roles as wives and mothers. These definitions of gender roles carry through to modern times and the role of women has recently become a major socio-political issue. We shall look at several main aspects of the social structure.

There are four defined stages to a person's life. Person refers to males of the four higher castes. The student stage is dedicated to formal study of the Vedas. The householder stage comes at the conclusion of studies, when a person marries, has children and engages in an occupation appropriate to his caste. The next stage is the retiree, when, as a grandfather, a person's responsibility is to share his knowledge of the world, the obligations to caste and his wisdom with the younger generations. The final stage is the acknowledgement of death. In this stage the person severs all ties with the previous life and turns his attention to spiritual study and preparation for death. This is an ideal progression which is not followed by all Indians, but is the respected way to live.

Related to, but not necessarily synchronized with these stages, are the four goals of life, kama, artha, dharma, and moksha. Kama focuses on pleasure, sensory desire and fulfillment. Artha focuses on success and material well being. Dharma focuses on duty, social contribution, and community. Moksha is union with the ultimate.

There are four methods (yogas) for accomplishing spiritual fruition. Dhyana is the path of meditation, in which one unites with Brahman through ecstatic experiences. Jnana is the path in which union with Brahman is achieved through the inspiration and insight gained in reading scripture. Karma is the path in which the activities of life provide the doorway to spiritual union if these activities are divorced from any reward. Bhakti is the path which is intensely focused devotion to a particular god, expressed through ritual.

No other civilization saw, appreciated, and classified so precisely the full spectrum of human personality types…an achievement that has earned for India – the title of the world's introspective psychologist. The key to this psychological perceptiveness is her recognition to the extent to which people will differ and the degree to which these differences are to be respected.
